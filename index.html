Morality at the Box Office

Exploring narratives in 980 blockbuster films, 1977–2025.

Cédric Rötheli

In the hope that my bachelor thesis would not simply disappear into a PDF and gather dust on a hard drive, I created this website. I should warn you: the thesis sits far enough outside the usual box of economics that, at times, you may be able to see the whole box office.

So, what is the idea?

Stories do more than entertain. They help organize attention, shape how we interpret events, and can ultimately influence beliefs and behavior. This idea sits at the heart of narrative economics: if stories affect how people understand the world, then the narratives that repeatedly reach millions of people are worth looking at. Commercially successful films are a particularly interesting case. They compete in an uncertain market for attention, are amplified through word of mouth and social influence, and the most successful among them achieve an unusually broad cultural reach.

But there is an obvious problem: how do you measure a story? Stories are complex, multidimensional objects, and reducing them to numbers inevitably means focusing on only part of what they contain. Recent advances in computational text analysis and large language models make it possible to approach this problem by identifying recurring narrative structures rather than trying to quantify an entire story at once. My thesis does this using plot synopses and subtitle material to examine how commercially successful films organize their central conflicts.

Morality at the Box Office explores the 20 highest-grossing films worldwide in every year from 1977 to 2025. Across 980 films, it asks a small set of deceptively simple questions: Who is threatened? Who protects them? How socially distant are they from the hero? And how large is the conflict? Together, these questions provide a way of comparing how films organize harm, protection, and moral concern across almost five decades.

The project draws inspiration from the concept of moral universalism developed by Benjamin Enke and co-authors: broadly, the distinction between moral concern that is concentrated on close in-groups and concern that extends across greater social distance. The measures used here are not a direct measure of moral universalism. Rather, they are a narrative analogue of that idea — a way of asking how broadly a film extends concern through the people who are threatened, the distance between them and the hero, and the scale of the conflict around them.
